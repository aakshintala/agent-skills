Open the content up so anyone can understand it the first time they read it — without losing substance, nuance or precision. Open up, not dumb down. Based on the GOV.UK A to Z style guide (guidance.publishing.service.gov.uk), but write in American English spelling (organize, color) and American conventions (dates as "June 4, 2026", a final period inside quotation marks). Applies to reports, research write-ups, guidance and any prose meant to be read; when writing a report, default to this style.

## Content design

- Start from the user need: what the reader needs to do or decide, not what you want to say.
- Front-load everything — document, section, paragraph, sentence. Inverted pyramid: conclusion first, then detail, then background.
- One idea per sentence, one topic per paragraph. Two ideas in a sentence means split it.
- Be specific and concrete: the number, the name, the date. Cut vague abstractions ("a range of", "going forward", "in terms of").
- Cut everything that does not add meaning. Shorter is clearer.

## Plain English

Keep all the substance and precision; strip out jargon, long sentences, abstract nouns and tangled structure. A non-specialist and an expert should both grasp it on first read.

- Active voice, saying who does what: "We reviewed the data", not "The data was reviewed".
- Sentences about 15 to 20 words, never more than about 25. Short paragraphs.
- Everyday words: use (not utilise/leverage), help (not facilitate/empower), work with (not collaborate/liaise/engage with), make or provide (not deliver), about (not in relation to), so (not in order to), start/end/buy/enough (not commence/terminate/purchase/sufficient), solve or fix (not tackle/combat), effect on (not impact on — never use impact as a verb).
- Address the reader as "you"; write about yourself as "we". Write "disabled people", not "the disabled".
- Contractions are fine for warmth (we'll, you'll), except negative ones: write "cannot", not "can't"; avoid "should've", "could've", "would've".

## Formatting

- Plain words and structure carry the meaning; bold and italics only to name a literal interface element in an instruction (select **Save**). Single quotation marks for scheme and document titles, not italics.
- Sentence case everywhere — headings, titles, table headers. Capitalise proper nouns only.
- Headings: front-loaded, under about 65 characters, unique and descriptive, no full stop, dash, slash or question mark.
- Bullets: lead-in line ending in a colon; each bullet lowercase, one idea, no "and"/"or" or semicolons at line ends, no final full stop unless the bullet is a full sentence.
- Numbered lists only for sequences the reader follows in order; steps are full sentences ending in a full stop.
- Links: descriptive text that says where the link goes, key words first, meaningful out of context.
- Write out Latin abbreviations: "for example" not "eg", "that is" not "ie", "such as" not "etc".
- Write "and", not "&" (registered names and logos excepted).
- Numbers: "one" spelled out, numerals from 2 up. "50%" with numerals. "£75" or "£75.50" (no decimals unless pence). "£5 million", not "£5m". Ranges with "to": "10 to 20", "Monday to Friday".
- Dates as "June 4, 2026"; ranges as "June 4 to 8". Times as "10am to 11.30am"; use "midday" and "midnight".
- No FAQs, no exclamation marks, no ALL CAPS for emphasis.

## Self-check before finishing

1. Is the single most important thing first?
2. Could a non-expert understand every sentence on first read?
3. Is every sentence active, short and one idea?
4. Is all bold/italic emphasis, jargon, Latin abbreviation and marketing language gone?
5. Is everything sentence case, with descriptive headings and links?
6. Could any more words be cut without losing meaning? Cut them.

The "no bold" and formatting rules apply to the prose you produce. Code, data tables and direct quotations keep their own conventions; markdown headings and lists are structure, not emphasis.
